A guide for parents

Driving Distance by Age and Course Length

“How far should my child hit the ball at this age?” “Is this tournament course too long for my child?” Using the Longleaf Tee System, which is widely used in junior golf in the United States, this guide explains distance guidelines and then compares them with course lengths at Japanese junior tournaments.

All distances are in yards (1 yard = about 0.91 m). Japanese golf courses and tournaments also use yards.

Key points

  1. Think in terms of your child’s current carry, not age
  2. Major Japanese tournaments play a little longer than the U.S. guidelines
  3. Check the course length of each event and build experience on courses your child can reach

How this guide is organized

STEP1

Start from driver carry

Distance has two parts. Carry is how far the ball flies in the air, and roll is how far it rolls after landing. Together they make the total distance (Fig. 1).

The Longleaf Tee System was created by the foundations of the American Society of Golf Course Architects (ASGCA Foundation) and U.S. Kids Golf as a way to choose the tee that suits a player’s distance. It is used at U.S. Kids Golf tournaments.

It explains that if you know a player’s driver carry, you can estimate the distance of every other club. Each club carries a nearly constant percentage of the driver, and those percentages are remarkably similar from beginners to skilled players. The relationship was checked by measuring players of all levels with a launch monitor (TrackMan).

STEP2

Guidelines by age

U.S. Kids Golf, a junior golf organization in the United States, uses the Longleaf Tee System to set the tee for each age group at its tournaments. Each tee number is the driver carry the organization assumes for children of that age. Applying that number to the table in Step 1 gives a guideline for each age.

This chart is not an average. It is a guideline the organization set as the basis for course setup. U.S. Kids Golf says it set the lengths using data collected at its own academies. Roll also changes a lot with ground firmness and slope.

Girls are set one tee forward of boys (25 yards less carry). In Japan’s national fitness survey, though, the strength gap between boys and girls is small in elementary school and grows in junior high (grip strength: grade 5 boys 16.0 kg, girls 15.8 kg; grade 8 boys 29.0 kg, girls 23.2 kg).

STEP3

Children differ a great deal

Fig. 4 is only a guideline. At the same age, distance differs greatly from child to child. Here are four things from research that parents should know.

In short: don’t worry if your child hits shorter than the table. And if they hit longer, they don’t need to play the same length as older children. Base decisions on your child’s current distance, not their age.

STEP4

Course length that suits each distance

The Longleaf Tee System shows, for each tee, the course length that suits a given driver carry. The idea is that each step up in tee (25 yards more carry) suits a course about 600 yards longer over 18 holes. In this guide, “one step” means one of these.

STEP5

Course lengths and age divisions at Japanese tournaments

Japan’s school year starts on April 1, and many tournaments group players by school grade. A grade 3 child is 8 at the start of the school year and turns 9 during it; grade 7 is the first year of junior high (中1). See How to enter a junior golf tournament in Japan for the full grade and birth-date table.

Major Japanese tournaments play a little longer than U.S. Kids Golf

Tournaments also group ages differently

Tournaments in this section (unofficial English names, with the Japanese names you will see in the guidelines): JGAI Cup (JGAIカップ, Ichihara Junior Golf Association); JJGA Ryo Ishikawa Cup (石川遼カップ ジュニアゴルフチャンピオンシップ); JLPGA All-Japan Elementary School Tournament qualifiers (全日本小学生ゴルフトーナメント 地区予選); Kanto Elementary School Championship (関東小学生ゴルフ大会, KGA); Japan Junior Championship (日本ジュニアゴルフ選手権, JGA).

Working out the right length for your child

Convert the length of the tournament you want to enter into “the carry it suits” using Fig. 6, and compare it with your child’s current carry. The JJGA ages 8–9 division (around 4,500 yards), for example, suits a 150-yard carry. For a child who carries 125 yards it is a little long, and you can expect many par 4s they can’t reach in two.

STEP6

How parents can use this

A guideline is just a guideline. The numbers here are not for comparing children or rushing them. What matters is building experience and enjoying the game on courses that suit your child’s current distance.
