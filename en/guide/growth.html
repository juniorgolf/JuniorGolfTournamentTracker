For families living in Japan

Golf teaches what textbooks can’t.

Courtesy, coping with disappointment, the courage to decide for yourself.
18 holes teach them all.

And your time together as a family grows, too.

A day of junior golf is full of moments that build a child’s character and abilities. This page introduces seven strengths that junior golf helps children develop, and the time it gives families together, along with recent research from the US, Europe and elsewhere.

Sport helps children grow inside

A 2026 review of 189 studies of children aged 5 to 17 found that children who play sport tend to have higher self-esteem and resilience, and stronger connections with their peers [1]. A 2025 Swedish analysis of studies that followed children for years also found that children who kept playing sport tended to have less anxiety and low mood, and better mental health [2].

Higher self-esteemChildren tend to value themselves more [1]
More resilienceThey tend to recover better from mistakes and hard times [1]
Less anxietyChildren who keep playing tend to have less anxiety and low mood [2]

Three things that make golf special

Children and adults on equal terms

Golf lets players of different abilities compete fairly. The handicap system was designed so that players of any ability, gender or nationality can compete fairly on any course in the world [3]. Children can play from shorter distances suited to their age, so they can have a real match with parents and grandparents.

Thinking before every shot

The basic principle of golf is to play the ball as it lies [4]. Children read the wind, slope and distance, and decide how to play each shot themselves.

A sport for the whole family, for life

From children to grandparents, everyone can play the same course together. International experts agreed unanimously that golf offers opportunities for people of different generations to spend time together [5].

Seven strengths junior golf builds

Connecting with others

1Friends beyond school and neighborhood

At a tournament, children spend several hours on the course in the same group as players they have never met. As they move up from regional events to national and overseas tournaments, they make friends across Japan and around the world.

ResearchChildren who play sport tend to feel more connected to their peers and to have stronger social skills. Researchers see friendships and support from the people around them as a central reason sport is good for children’s mental health [1].

Try sayingAfter a tournament, ask: “What did you talk about with the kids in your group?”

2Good manners and consideration

The very first rule of golf asks every player to play with integrity (follow the rules, apply penalties to yourself and be honest), to be considerate of others and to take care of the course [4]. Greeting the group at the start and finish, repairing your marks and calling your own penalties are all part of the game itself.

ResearchIn a study of The First Tee, a US program that teaches life skills through golf, participants were rated higher than children in other activities on life skills such as greeting people and getting along with others. Over three years, they kept using those skills outside golf, including at school [6].

Try sayingWhen your child greets their group before and after the round, praise them for it.

Inner strength

3Bouncing back after a bad shot

Even professionals hit only a few shots per round exactly as planned. After a mistake, the next shot comes right away. Golf lets children practise putting frustration aside and looking ahead dozens of times in a single day.

ResearchChildren who play sport tend to be more resilient. Experiments that compared two groups of children also suggest that sport can build resilience and the ability to manage emotions [1].

Try sayingAfter a miss, ask a forward-looking question: “What will you do with the next shot?”

4Confidence when others are watching

The first tee shot is hit in front of the other players, parents and tournament officials. Your name is called, you greet the group and step up when it is your turn. Even in elementary school, children gain experience performing in front of others.

ResearchA UK analysis of 14 experiments (2020) found that practising under pressure helped people perform better when it really counted, for beginners and experienced athletes alike [7].

Try sayingBefore talking about the result, recognise that they stepped up: “You were nervous, but you hit it well.”

5Focus when it matters

Before each shot, players pick a target and focus. After hitting, they relax and walk. Golfers repeat this switch dozens of times a day, outdoors on the grass.

ResearchPhysical activity that makes children think as they move improved self-control and working memory (a review of 23 experiments with about 2,900 children, 2024) [8]. Time in nature has also been linked to better attention in children (a review of 12 studies, mostly from Europe, 2022) [9]. Golf offers both.

Try sayingWork out a pre-shot routine together. It becomes a signal to switch on their focus.

Growing on their own

6Confidence that grows with practice

In golf, progress shows up as a number: the score. Children can set goals like breaking 100 or 90 and enjoy seeing their practice pay off.

ResearchChildren who play sport tend to have higher self-esteem. Researchers see the feeling of “I can do this” as one of the ways sport builds it [1].

Try sayingFind one thing that improved since last time, together. It can be the number of putts or out-of-bounds shots.

7Thinking and deciding for themselves

No two situations are ever the same. Children read the wind, slope and distance, then choose their club and shot. Some tournaments do not allow parents who are not caddies to give advice during play [10], so once on the course, children play on their own judgement.

ResearchA review of 29 studies from the US, Europe and elsewhere (2024) found that when parents let children make choices and respect their feelings, children are more likely to take up sport on their own initiative [11].

Try sayingAsk why they chose that club in that situation. The trick is to listen without correcting the answer.

Family time grows, too

Drives to the range and lessons, trips to tournaments, talking over the round on the way home: junior golf naturally gives parents and children more time together. Families can work toward a goal like “break 100 next time” as a team. And golf is a sport where parents and grandparents can play the same course with their child [5].

More family conversation

A 2025 review of 43 studies by Canadian researchers found that families of physically active children tended to be closer and talk more. The link with family communication was especially clear for teenagers [12].

On the drive home, let your child lead

A 2025 UK study recorded conversations in the car on the way home from junior tournaments. When children brought up the match themselves and parents followed their lead, relaxed conversations lasted longer, whether they had won or lost [13].

Three ways to help these strengths grow

  1. Connect what they learn in golf to everyday life. In a US study (2020), children in programs that deliberately taught life skills used skills such as managing emotions outside sport more often [6, 14]. Put the link into words: “Reset for the test the way you reset for the next shot.”
  2. Support them, but let them lead. Parents’ encouragement and support are linked to children’s enjoyment and motivation. The best involvement is neither too much nor too little [11].
  3. Enjoy other sports and outdoor play too. A Dutch study that followed about 880 children (2024) found that children who played both team and individual sports tended to have better focus and mental flexibility [15]. Mixing golf with other sports is a good idea.
